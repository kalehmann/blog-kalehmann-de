Recently, I moved into a new shared flat.
Unfortunately, that meant I could no longer install a random wire antenna for
amateur radio.
For the past few weeks now, I have been looking for an alternative antenna setup
to get on the air from my new home.
Finally, I have settled on the concept of building a magnetic loop antenna.

This post documents the design of such a magnetic loop antenna for indoor QRP
operation as well as its assembly process using only inexpensive parts from the
local hardware store.

[![The magnetic loop antenna on the ceiling with a cat looking up to it.][loop_antenna]][loop_antenna]

> **Note of warning:** Magnetic loop antennas tend to reach thousands of volts
> across the capacitor terminals even at QRP operation.
> If this post inspires you to build your own loop, please proceed with care and
> with caution.
>
> Do not touch a magnetic loop antenna during transmission and keep a safe
> distance.

## What is a magnetic loop antenna?

A magnetic loop antenna is basically an LC circuit consisting of the main loop
as inductor and a variable capacitor for tuning to the operating frequency.
Since the impedance of the LC circuit at resonance will be far lower than the
50 Ω impedance that is common in amateur radio, a smaller loop - called the
coupling loop - is placed inside the main loop.
Together, the two loops act as a transformer for impedance matching.

{% include_relative embedded/2026-11-magloop/lc_circuit.svg %}

The antenna is called a magnetic loop because electromagnetic fields are the
combination of a time-varying electric field component <math><mi>E</mi></math>
and a magnetic field component <math><mi>H</mi></math>, which are perpendicular
to each other mutually coupled, thus propagating electromagnetic waves through
space.
Unlike an antenna like a standard electrical dipole, whose near field is
dominated by its electric field - the  magnetic loop antenna with its primary
loop as an inductor, has a near field dominated by its magnetic component.
Although it should be mentioned, that in the far field both antennas produce
electromagnetic waves consisting of perpendicular <math><mi>E</mi></math>
and <math><mi>H</mi></math> components.

Furthermore, the voltage across the terminals of the variable capacitor can
easily exceed a thousand volts, even with a low transmission power of 5 watts.
Therefore the capacitor needs to be able to withstand a high voltage.
High voltage air variable capacitors - where the plates are separated only by
air - can be bought on the internet.
But they are not as common as they used to be and quite expensive.
However, they are not that complex mechanically.
Therefore, I decided to build one myself.

## How to build an air variable capacitor?

An air variable capacitor consists of two groups of plates.
One group is fixed - called the _stator_ and the other group is placed on a
rotating shaft - called the _rotor_.
By slowly rotating the rotor over the stator, the overlapping area between the
two groups of plates can be increased or decreased - thus varying the
capacitance.

The dielectric strength of air is given as
approximately <math displaystyle="true"><mn>3</mn><mfrac><mtext>kV</mtext><mtext>mm</mtext></mfrac></math>
under ideal conditions in textbooks.
However, the actual dielectric strength can vary and sharp edges may
significantly lower the breakdown voltage.
Therefore, as a rule of thumb, I want to have at least one millimeter of distance
between the plates for every kilovolt in my design to be on the safe side.

Next, to avoid making an electrical connection to a moving part of the capacitor,
such as the rotor, I used two groups of stator plates which are electrically
separated and use each group as a terminal.
Electrically, this is equivalent to putting two identical capacitors in series,
which requires more plates as the total capacitance <math><mi>C</mi></math>
becomes halved.
However at the same time, in a series circuit of two equal ideal capacitors,
the voltage at each capacitor equals half the total voltage,
thus also lowering the required voltage tolerance for my DIY capacitor.

In the end, I decided to cut 10 semicircular rotor plates from a 0.5 mm thick
sheet of aluminum and placed them on threaded rods, separated by hex nuts.
Next, the stators were cut as rectangular plates and also separated by nuts.
The overlapping area between each stator and rotor plate is about
25 <math><msup><mtext>cm</mtext><mn>2</mn></msup></math> when fully closed.
Using the nuts as spacers gives a distance of about 2.5 millimeters between
the plates.
Furthermore, I cut additional spacers from an aluminum pipe for the threaded
rods that do not hold plates, but serve only as stabilizers for the
construction.
Finally, the top and bottom plates of the capacitor were cut from acrylic glass.

[![The partially assembled variable capacitor with a set of plates next to it][capacitor_plates]][capacitor_plates]{:.image-left}
[![The fully assembled variable capacitor][variable_capacitor]][variable_capacitor]{:.image-right}

After assembling the capacitor, I cut a little cogwheel from acrylic glass and
mounted it on the rotor, to have some more torque when turning the rotor.
Then, I calibrated my NanoVNA from 1 to 30 MHz and connected the capacitor to
it to get a rough estimate of its actual capacitance.

[![A NanoVNA connected to the capacitor displaying a Smith chart][capacitance_measurement]][capacitance_measurement]

While I do not trust the results too much due to inaccuracies introduced
by the VNA itself and the test leads, I measured the capacitance to range
from about 20 pF fully open to 60 pF fully closed.
This differs quite a bit from my previously calculated capacitance of about
45 pF fully closed.
However, I consider this still a reasonable deviation given the additional
capacitance introduced by the threaded rods and spacers, as well as inaccuracies
in the construction.

## How to assemble the antenna?

With the capacitor finished, building the loop was easy.
I got all the parts except the coax for connecting the antenna to my transceiver
from the local hardware store.
The full bill of materials for the antenna, including the variable capacitor, is
as follows:

| Material                              | Price       |
|---------------------------------------|-------------|
| Aluminum plate 250 x 500 x 0.5 mm     |  4.45 €     |
| Threaded rod M4 x 1000 mm             |  0.80 €     |
| 100 M4 hex nuts (3 mm thick)          |  2.55 €     |
| Acrylic glass plate 250 x 500 x 4 mm  |  7.95 €     |
| Aluminum beam 25 x 2 mm, 2.6 meters   |  8.45 €     |
| Aluminum pipe Ø 6 mm, 1 m             |  1.80 €     |
| 2 cylinder bolts M4 16 mm             |  0.66 €     |
| 2 wingnuts M4                         |  0.44 €     |
| 1 meter of H07 V-K 1x10 mm² wire      |  1.98 €     |
| **Total**                             | **29.08 €** |

At just under 30 Euros, that seems like a reasonable price to me.

For the construction, first I bent the main loop out of an aluminum beam.
The longest available beam was 2.6 meters long.
That gives a loop diameter of about 80 centimeters.
With a cross section of 25 x 2 mm, bending the beam was easy.
In fact, I already bent it at the hardware store as a ring was easier to
transport than the long beam.
Finally at home, I drilled two 4-millimeter holes at the ends of the beam for
connecting the terminals of my variable capacitor.

Next, I took about 60 centimeters of
a 10 <math><msup><mtext>mm</mtext><mn>2</mn></msup></math> cable for the coupling
loop and connected it to a BNC-to-screw-terminal adapter.
Then I placed the coupling loop inside the main loop, turned the capacitor half
open and connected the NanoVNA to the coupling loop.

Sweeping from 1 to 30 MHz, I could already see the resonance frequency, but the
lowest SWR was still above 3:1.
To improve the SWR match, I reduced the length of the coupling loop a bit and
used duct tape to secure it tightly to the top of the main loop.

And that's it - the magnetic loop antenna is finished:
[![The fully assembled antenna with both loops clearly visible][loop_assembled]][loop_assembled]

After assembling the two loops and wiring up the capacitor, I used some leftover
rope to attach the antenna to the ceiling of my room.

## How does the loop perform?

Well, I am satisfied with the antenna's performance.
Turning the variable capacitor from fully open to fully closed tunes the
antenna over the range from 16 to 30 MHz.
This includes the 10, 12, 15 and 17 meter amateur radio bands.
